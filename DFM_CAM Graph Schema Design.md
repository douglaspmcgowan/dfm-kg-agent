# **Foundational Graph Schema and Semantic Ontology for Automated Mechanical Design for Manufacturing and Machining Pipelines**

The integration of artificial intelligence into mechanical engineering requires a fundamental paradigm shift in how manufacturing data is structured, stored, and queried. Historically, machine learning projects within factory environments have failed not due to algorithmic deficiencies, but because the data they consume lacks structure, semantic meaning, and continuity.1 Traditional relational databases and flat tabular structures are fundamentally ill-equipped to capture the intertwined, multidimensional behaviors of a manufacturing ecosystem. A cycle time or a dimensional tolerance is merely an isolated numerical value unless it is semantically linked to an operator, a specific material state, a historical machine alarm, and a distinct geometric feature.1 To move beyond rudimentary automation toward truly autonomous, AI-driven process planning, the industry must transition to a Manufacturing Knowledge Graph (MKG).

A knowledge graph models data as a collection of interconnected nodes (entities) and directed edges (relationships), creating a network explicitly designed to mirror real-world physical and operational semantics.2 Unlike rigid relational databases where schema modifications are computationally expensive and complex queries result in sluggish table joins, a graph database provides a flexible, "schema-lite" architecture.2 This allows an AI agent to traverse deep relationships—from a high-level product assembly down to the microscopic chip load of a specific end mill—in milliseconds.5

This comprehensive report establishes a vendor-agnostic graph schema architecture for executing a mechanical product Design for Manufacturing (DFM) and computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) pipeline. Rather than relying on the proprietary, closed-source architectures of commercial software (e.g., Autodesk Fusion 360, Mastercam), this schema is anchored in universal engineering standards and open ontologies. It provides an exhaustive lexicon of design and machining terminology, structured specifically to serve as a foundational reference guide for an AI agent tasked with building, populating, and reasoning over a manufacturing knowledge graph.

## **Ontological Foundations for a Vendor-Agnostic Schema**

To ensure interoperability across heterogeneous software ecosystems, the graph schema must be governed by formalized ontologies. Ontologies provide the logical blueprints of knowledge, structuring abstract concepts into machine-readable classes, instances, attributes, and axioms.6 In the context of manufacturing, several key standards and semantic frameworks provide the taxonomic scaffolding required for the graph.

The Manufacturing Service Description Language (MSDL) serves as the primary upper ontology for this schema. MSDL is an OWL-based (Web Ontology Language) framework designed for the formal representation of manufacturing capabilities.7 Crucially, MSDL decomposes manufacturing resources into five hierarchical levels of abstraction: supplier-level, shop-level, machine-level, device-level, and process-level.7 By mapping this hierarchy into the graph, an AI agent can execute granular supplier discovery and resource allocation, dynamically routing a specific geometric feature to a capable machine on the shop floor.7 Complementing MSDL is the MASON (Manufacturing's Semantics Ontology) framework, which provides a comprehensive semantic net specifically for mapping physical resources, operations, and raw materials.9

To bridge the gap between design geometry and physical manufacturing, the schema must abandon the legacy ISO 6983 standard (G-code) in favor of ISO 14649, universally known as STEP-NC.11 Traditional G-code breaks the digital thread; it is a low-level language that only dictates tool tip trajectories and axis motions, completely stripping away the design intent of the part.12 A CNC machine reading G-code does not comprehend that it is machining a "pocket" or a "hole"; it merely executes a series of coordinates.

STEP-NC resolves this limitation by providing an object-oriented data model that facilitates bi-directional communication between CAD, CAM, and CNC systems.13 At the core of the STEP-NC ontology is the Workingstep entity. A workingstep explicitly links a Manufacturing\_Feature (defining "what to make," such as a planar face or closed pocket) with a Machining\_Operation (defining "how to make it," such as rough milling or fine boring).13 By adopting the STEP-NC Application Reference Model (ARM) alongside ISO 10303-224 (which standardizes the definition of machining features for process planning), the graph schema inherently preserves design intent throughout the entire manufacturing pipeline.16

Furthermore, to manage the geometric relationships within complex assemblies, the schema leverages mereotopology—the formalized study of parts and the boundaries connecting them.18 Utilizing multi-dimensional mereotopological ontologies allows the AI agent to logically express physical connections, spatial parthood, and shape boundaries, enabling automated generative design and configuration pruning.18

## **Graph Schema Architecture: Node Typologies and Attributes**

In a Labeled Property Graph (LPG) environment like Neo4j, data is structured through nodes that hold key-value pairs representing specific properties.19 For the DFM and CAM pipeline, the AI agent must instantiate distinct node labels to categorize the physical and conceptual elements of the manufacturing lifecycle.

### **Design and Product Nodes**

The digital representation of the physical object begins with the Part node. This node serves as the central hub for the product structure. Its properties must capture high-level metadata, including the Part\_ID, Bounding\_Box\_X, Bounding\_Box\_Y, Bounding\_Box\_Z, Total\_Volume, and Target\_Tolerance\_Class.20

Attached to the Part node are Feature nodes, which represent the localized geometric structures extracted from the Boundary Representation (B-Rep) CAD model.22 The categorization of features is extensive and must adhere to standard STEP-NC definitions. A feature labeled Pocket requires properties such as Length, Width, Depth, Internal\_Corner\_Radius, and Floor\_Flatness.24 A feature labeled Hole requires properties dictating Diameter, Depth, Is\_Through (Boolean), and Tolerance\_Requirement.24 The granular parameterization of these feature nodes is what enables the AI to perform automated geometric reasoning.

The physical substance of the part is represented by the Material node. Material selection profoundly impacts every downstream CAM parameter and DFM constraint.26 The AI agent must populate this node with precise engineering properties derived from materials science databases. Critical properties include Ultimate\_Tensile\_Strength\_MPa, Yield\_Strength\_MPa, Hardness\_Brinell, Thermal\_Conductivity, Elastic\_Modulus\_GPa, and a normalized Machinability\_Index.27 For instance, machining titanium or high-performance polymers like PEEK requires vastly different calculations than machining 6061 aluminum due to variations in hardness and thermal deformation thresholds.26

### **Manufacturing Resource Nodes**

The physical capabilities of the factory floor are modeled through equipment and tooling nodes. The Machine node encapsulates the kinematic and power limits of the CNC equipment. Properties for a milling machine must include Axis\_Count (e.g., 3, 4, or 5-axis), Max\_Spindle\_Speed\_RPM, Max\_Feed\_Rate\_MM\_MIN, Spindle\_Power\_KW, Spindle\_Taper (e.g., BT40, CAT40), Positioning\_Accuracy\_MM, and Controller\_Type (e.g., Fanuc, Siemens).30

The Tool node represents the cutting implement required to remove material. To ensure accuracy, the AI agent should ingest tool properties aligned with the ISO 13399 standard, which provides a universal language for cutting tool data representation.32 Properties must include Tool\_Type (e.g., Flat End Mill, Ball Nose, Drill, Tap), Cutting\_Diameter, Number\_of\_Flutes, Flute\_Length, Overall\_Length, Corner\_Radius, and Tool\_Material (e.g., Solid Carbide, High-Speed Steel, Polycrystalline Diamond).33

Workholding is a critical, yet frequently overlooked, aspect of the machining pipeline. The Fixture node defines how the part is secured during cutting. Accurate positioning and extreme rigidity are required to prevent part slippage and vibration under heavy cutting loads.36 Fixture properties outline the Fixture\_Type (e.g., Machine Vise, Soft Jaws, Collet, Tombstone, Vacuum Plate), Max\_Clamping\_Force, and Material\_Compatibility.38 Custom 3D-printed soft jaws or nylon collet pads are often required for complex geometries, requiring the AI to cross-reference the fixture constraints with the part's external bounding box.38

### **Process Planning and Parameter Nodes**

Process planning involves mapping the sequence of events required to transform the raw stock into the final part. The ProcessPlan node dictates the macroscopic routing, such as moving from a Turning operation to a Milling operation.41 The Operation node represents the specific machining action applied to a feature, categorized into strategies such as Roughing, Finishing, Adaptive\_Clearing, or Contour\_Profiling.42

Bridging the geometry and the action is the Workstep node, adopting the STEP-NC paradigm. The workstep aggregates the feature, the operation, and the necessary parameters into an executable unit.44 Finally, the Parameter node stores the specific physics calculations required for the cut. This node contains dynamic values such as Cutting\_Speed\_Vc, Spindle\_Speed\_N, Feed\_Rate\_Vf, Feed\_Per\_Tooth\_Fz, Axial\_Depth\_of\_Cut\_Ap, and Radial\_Depth\_of\_Cut\_Ae.45

## **Relational Topology: Structuring the Semantic Edges**

The true analytical power of a knowledge graph resides in its edges, which provide the semantic context necessary for an AI agent to traverse the network and deduce manufacturing plans.47 In an LPG, these directed edges can also carry properties, making them ideal for representing temporal or conditional relationships.48

The structural hierarchy of the product is established using the HAS\_FEATURE relationship, pointing from a Part node to its constituent Feature nodes.41 The physical composition is defined by the MADE\_OF edge linking the Part to a specific Material. To establish geometric relationships between features—critical for identifying thin walls or intersecting holes—the schema employs ADJACENT\_TO or INTERSECTS edges, carrying distance or angular properties.20

Process planning logic relies on sequential and dependency mapping. The REQUIRES\_OPERATION edge links a Feature to the necessary Operation nodes. To define the manufacturing timeline, the PRECEDES edge links sequential Workstep or Operation nodes. This sequential mapping allows the AI agent to aggregate cycle times and identify critical path bottlenecks.

Resource allocation is managed by the MACHINED\_ON relationship, mapping an Operation to a specific Machine, and the USES\_TOOL relationship, connecting the Operation to a Tool. The workholding strategy utilizes the SECURED\_BY relationship, pointing from an Operation to a Fixture.

Most critically for automated manufacturability analysis, the schema employs CONSTRAINED\_BY or EVALUATED\_AGAINST relationships. These edges link Feature, Part, or Operation nodes directly to DFM\_Rule nodes. When the AI agent traverses these edges, it triggers executable logic to validate the physical possibility and economic viability of the proposed design.49

## **Executing Design for Manufacturing (DFM) via Graph Traversals**

Design for Manufacturability (DFM) is an engineering methodology that forces the consideration of manufacturing constraints, material properties, and production capabilities early in the design phase.50 The fundamental goal is to simplify geometry, reduce cycle times, eliminate specialized tooling, and minimize the risk of part failure during production.24 Over 70% of a product's final cost is determined during the design stage, making DFM validation critical.50

In a traditional ecosystem, DFM relies on human intuition, spreadsheets, and disconnected software rules.54 In this graph schema, DFM principles are encoded as executable traversal queries. The AI agent navigates the relationships between geometry, materials, and tooling to identify constraint violations programmatically.

### **Deep Cavity and Tool Deflection Constraints**

A fundamental law of CNC milling is that cutting tools are subjected to severe lateral forces. If a cavity (such as a pocket or slot) is too deep relative to its width, a long, thin end mill must be used. Extended tooling is highly susceptible to deflection, vibration (chatter), poor surface finish, and catastrophic breakage.24 The standard industry heuristic dictates that the depth of a pocket or slot should not exceed four times its width, and hole depths should not exceed four times their diameter.24

The AI agent executes this DFM check by traversing from a Feature node. If the node is labeled Pocket, the query evaluates the internal properties: IF Pocket.Depth \> (4 \* Pocket.Width) THEN RETURN Violation. If a violation is flagged, the agent can traverse the graph to identify alternative strategies. It may seek a Machine node capable of multi-axis repositioning to attack the feature from a different angle, or it may suggest splitting the component into two separate parts (moving from DFM to Design for Assembly, or DFA).50

### **Internal Corner Radii and Tool Interpolation**

Because CNC milling relies on rotating cylindrical cutters, machining a perfectly sharp, 90-degree internal vertical corner is physically impossible.24 Designers often mistakenly leave sharp corners in CAD models, which forces manufacturers to either use costly secondary operations like Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM) or stop production to request a design change.55 DFM principles demand that all internal corners feature radii that accommodate standard tooling profiles.24

Within the graph, the AI agent maps the Feature (e.g., Pocket) to the Tool node via the USES\_TOOL relationship. The traversal checks if the Pocket.Internal\_Corner\_Radius is less than the Tool.Radius. Furthermore, a highly optimized DFM query will verify that the pocket radius is slightly *larger* than the tool radius.24 If the radii match exactly, the tool achieves maximum engagement (100% radial depth of cut) when it hits the corner, causing severe stress spikes and chatter. By ensuring the feature radius is larger, the tool can gracefully interpolate through the corner.

### **Wall Thickness and Thermal Warpage**

Thin walls represent a significant manufacturing risk. During machining, walls that are too thin lack the rigidity to withstand the lateral forces of the cutter, leading to breakage.24 Furthermore, the machining process generates intense heat and relieves internal material stresses, causing thin sections to warp and flex after the part is removed from the fixture.24 Standard DFM guidelines for CNC machining suggest maintaining wall thicknesses greater than 0.020 inches (0.51 mm), with a best practice of exceeding 0.030 inches (0.762 mm).33

The graph evaluates this risk by analyzing the Distance properties on ADJACENT\_TO edges between parallel Feature nodes (e.g., the web of material between two parallel pockets). The AI agent must also consult the Material node during this traversal. Plastic components, which have low thermal conductivity and low stiffness, are vastly more susceptible to thermal deformation than hardened steel.26 Consequently, the AI agent dynamically adjusts the minimum allowable wall thickness threshold based on the specific material properties retrieved from the graph.26

### **Text Engraving and Feature Accessibility**

Adding custom text, logos, or part numbers to a machined component significantly impacts cycle time and requires specialized micro-tooling.24 DFM rules recommend recessed text over raised text (which requires milling away all surrounding material) and specify exact dimensional limits based on material hardness.33 For plastics and soft metals, the minimum text stroke width should be 0.018 inches (0.457 mm) with a depth of 0.0118 inches (0.3 mm).33 For hard metals, the minimum width increases to 0.033 inches (0.838 mm) to accommodate stronger, thicker engraving tools.33 The graph query easily validates these parameters by evaluating the Feature properties against the Material hardness.

Additionally, the AI agent must scan for undercuts—features that are hidden or inaccessible from a standard vertical tool approach.33 Undercuts require specialized tools (like T-slot or lollipop cutters) or multi-axis machines, drastically increasing costs. The agent flags any feature whose geometry requires an approach vector that conflicts with the machine's primary kinematic axes.33

## **Mathematical Parameterization for Toolpath Generation**

Once a part passes the DFM validation phase, the AI agent transitions to the CAM pipeline, where it must calculate the precise physics required to safely and efficiently remove material. These parameters are dynamically generated by traversing the intersection of the Material, Tool, and Machine nodes, ultimately populating the Parameter node linked to each Operation.

For highly rigid industrial machines, large depths of cut are permissible. Conversely, for less rigid, hobby-grade routers, the AI agent must programmatically decrease the depth per pass while maintaining the calculated feed rates to reduce cutting forces and prevent chatter.59

### **Advanced Toolpath Strategies**

To optimize manufacturing efficiency, the AI agent maps specific Operation types to advanced toolpath algorithms. Modern CAM systems rely on highly sophisticated geometric calculations to minimize tool wear and reduce cycle times.43

For rapid bulk material removal (roughing), the agent selects Adaptive Clearing or Trochoidal Milling strategies.42 Rather than forcing a tool into heavy, full-width cuts that cause massive spikes in force, adaptive strategies utilize circular, looping motions with a highly controlled, dynamic stepover.46 This maintains a perfectly constant chip load on the tool, allowing the machinist to utilize the entire flute length (massive axial depth) without breaking the cutter.62

For facing flat surfaces, Zig-Zag or Parallel toolpaths are deployed to minimize tool retractions and optimize coverage.43 Contour or Profile toolpaths are utilized for precision finishing passes, strictly following the outline of the part geometry to achieve required dimensional tolerances.43 Finally, Rest Machining operations are automatically generated by the AI to calculate the exact volume of material left behind by larger roughing tools, safely targeting only the remaining stock with smaller cutters.62

GD\&T is a universal engineering language utilized on technical drawings to convey design intent, functional relationships, and acceptable geometric deviation.65 Within the graph, these terms act as quality constraints that dictate specific machining strategies and inspection protocols.

| Keyword / Terminology | Engineering Definition | Graph Schema Context & DFM Application |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |

| **Flatness** | A form control ensuring that all measured points on a specific surface reside strictly between two parallel planes. 65 | Critical for parts requiring vacuum fixturing or precise mating. Maps to Facing toolpath operations with low stepovers. 65 |
| **Circularity (Roundness)** | Dictates that the 2D cross-section of a cylindrical or spherical feature must lie between two concentric circles. 65 | Mapped to boring or precision turning operations. High circularity demands slow feed rates and rigid setups. 65 |

| **True Position** | A location control defining the exact, theoretically perfect coordinate of a feature (e.g., a hole center) and establishing a cylindrical tolerance zone around it. 65 | Fundamental for hole-making. The graph correlates tight true position with rigid Machine positioning accuracy and probing operations. 65 |
| **Profile of a Surface** | A 3D tolerance zone applied to complex, freeform surfaces or advanced curves, restricting dimensional deviation across the entire profile. 67 | Maps directly to complex 3D surfacing CAM operations using ball-nose end mills with minimal stepovers. 67 |
| **Datum** | A theoretically perfect plane, axis, or point that serves as the fixed anchor from which all other GD\&T dimensional tolerances are referenced. 55 | Defines the primary coordinate system (Zero Point) for the CAM setup. ## **Graph Pipeline Evolution and Continuous Data Integration**

The construction of the Manufacturing Knowledge Graph is not a static event; it requires dynamic data ingestion and continuous model evolution. Because manufacturing environments generate vast amounts of heterogeneous data—from rigid ERP databases and XML files to unstructured PDF tool datasheets and real-time IoT sensor logs—an automated ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) pipeline is required.83

Structured data from CAD systems (B-rep geometry, dimensional attributes) and CAM software (toolpath vectors) can be seamlessly integrated into the Neo4j graph using procedural programmatic methods or native GraphQL APIs.2 Natural Language Processing (NLP) models, specifically transformer architectures like Bi-LSTM-CRF, are employed to extract implicit rules, machine capabilities, and tooling configurations from unstructured texts and legacy process planning cards, mapping them directly into the established schema ontologies.41

As the digital pipeline evolves, version control for graph schemas becomes essential. Introducing new tool technologies, updating machine kinematic profiles, or modifying DFM constraint thresholds requires schema evolution.85 The pipeline must handle graph refactoring operations carefully, utilizing path-based testing to ensure that updating a specific Tool property does not inadvertently break the semantic traversal paths required for the DFM\_Rule validation engine.85 Furthermore, integrating this continuously updated MKG with advanced Large Language Models (LLMs) via GraphRAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) prevents AI hallucinations by grounding all automated reasoning in mathematically verifiable, graph-structured engineering physics.4

## **Conclusion**

The transition from isolated, tabular datasets to a semantically rich Manufacturing Knowledge Graph is essential for the realization of autonomous, AI-driven process planning. By architecting a schema anchored in universal standards—utilizing the MSDL hierarchy for resource abstraction, STEP-NC (ISO 14649\) for object-oriented process mapping, and ISO 13399 for tooling classification—the pipeline remains robust, scalable, and independent of proprietary software constraints.8

This graph schema transcends mere data storage; it acts as an executable computational engine. By codifying Design for Manufacturability heuristics as dynamic graph traversals, the AI agent can autonomously detect physical constraints—such as excessive tool deflection in deep cavities, catastrophic wall warpage, and inaccessible undercuts—long before physical manufacturing commences.24 Furthermore, by semantically linking Material, Tool, and Machine nodes, the graph calculates optimized cutting parameters based on foundational engineering physics, translating abstract geometry into highly efficient, collision-free toolpaths.45

The comprehensive lexicons established herein provide the necessary vocabulary for an AI agent to parse, navigate, and reason across the entirety of the mechanical engineering domain. By utilizing this structured property graph framework, manufacturers can bridge the historic disconnect between design intent and factory-floor execution, establishing a fully integrated, intelligent digital thread.
